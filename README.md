# Code, supporting data and supplementary materials for *Pedological classification as a key determinant of arable soil microbial communities*
Jeanne Durivage, Thiago Gumiere, Richard Hogue, Thomas Jeanne, Marc-Olivier Gasser, Mary-Cathrine Leewis, Marie-Élise Samson

Corresponding author: Marie-Élise Samson (marie-elise.samson@fsaa.ulaval.ca)

**This is part of a preprint. This repository will be updated uppon article publication.**

## Abstract

Climatic and soil-related variables, along with soil management practices, have 
been identified as key determinants of soil microbial composition and, 
consequently, soil ecosystem functions. While numerous biogeographic studies at 
local to continental scales have identified several variables such as pH, soil 
carbon quality and quantity and total precipitation as important predictors of
microbial composition and diversity, these observations have not been validated 
by similar biogeographic research within agricultural systems, limiting our 
ability to compare microbiomes from different cultivated sites. This 
observational study of topsoil microbial composition in Quebec's cash crop 
fields is one of the rare biogeographical studies conducted in arable land that 
investigates a wide range of soil, climate, and farming-related variables. In 
this study, the taxonomic and predictive carbon-related functional composition 
of microbial communities was investigated in 81 cultivated mineral topsoils 
under real conditions across 38,000 km2 in Quebec, Canada. Using multivariate 
statistics, we examined how two climatic variables (precipitation and growing 
degree-days), 9 soil-related variables (pedological classification, soil 
texture and structure, pH, carbon and nutrient concentrations), and 6 
agricultural management practices (crop diversity indexes, tillage intensities, 
organic fertilization type and compaction risk) influenced community variation 
(represented by the robust Aitchison beta diversity). Pedological 
classification, based on the Canadian System of Soil Classification, emerged as 
the strongest predictor of prokaryotic and fungal taxonomic diversity and 
predicted prokaryotic carbon cycling functional potential, whereas farming 
practices showed little relative influence. All six climate and soil-related 
variables that significantly explained microbial variance in the biogeographic 
analyses were also significantly different between pedological soil 
classifications. We also hypothesized that the strong predictive power of soil 
classification indicates that it serves as a proxy for additional key variables, 
such as redox status. Further taxonomic analyses revealed that stratifying 
samples by pedological groups provided a more nuanced understanding of the 
specific effects of organic fertilizer type and soil tillage intensity, with
distinct taxonomic families responding within each soil classification and some 
soils showing distinct sensitivities to these farming practices. This evidence 
supports integrating soil classification as a foundational framework for 
assessing microbial responses to farming practices across soil types and for 
monitoring the soil microbiome at large geographic scales.
